What to Play Between Innings: Stadium Music for Youth Baseball & Softball

Walk-up songs get the attention, but the silence between innings is longer. Here is what to play, when to cut it, and what the rules actually say.

Why between-innings music matters more than you think

Walk-up songs get all the attention, but they account for a few seconds per batter. The silence between innings is far longer, and it is the difference between a field that feels like an event and a field that feels like a practice. Parents notice it even when they cannot name it.

The bar is low, too. Most amateur fields play nothing at all between innings, so a team that plays anything sounds organised by comparison. You do not need a production; you need something in the air while the teams change sides.

How to time it without stepping on the game

The one rule that matters: the music serves the game, not the other way round. Start it once the half-inning is clearly over and cut it the moment the umpire is ready. Nobody has ever complained that the between-innings music stopped too early.

Break length varies enormously by league, level and whether anyone is warming up, so do not build a routine around a fixed number of seconds. Pick tracks you can fade out at any point rather than ones that need to reach a chorus to make sense, and keep the volume low enough that a coach can shout across the infield without competing with it.

A practical pattern that works: background music during the change, a short rally track if your side is mounting something, and nothing at all when the game is tight and late. Restraint reads as competence.

Rally & Crowd-Participation Tracks

These exist to make noise happen. Use them sparingly — a rally song loses its meaning if it plays every inning.

  1. “Seven Nation Army” — The White Stripes. The crowd chants it without being asked.
  2. “Cotton Eye Joe” — Rednex. The single most effective between-innings track in amateur ball.
  3. “Zombie Nation” — Kernkraft 400. Pure stadium noise. Works everywhere.
  4. “Sandstorm” — Darude. Build it under a rally and let it go.
  5. “Song 2” — Blur. Two minutes, all payoff.
  6. “Tequila” — The Champs. The whole bleacher shouts the word.
  7. “Shout” — The Isley Brothers. Built for a comeback inning.
  8. “Sweet Caroline” — Neil Diamond. Automatic sing-along in the seventh.
  9. “Don't Stop Believin'” — Journey. Everyone participates whether they mean to or not.
  10. “Livin' on a Prayer” — Bon Jovi. Halfway there, and the parents take over.

Background Music That Fills the Gaps

Lower stakes. These should sit under conversation rather than command attention, and every one is clean.

  1. “Uptown Funk” — Bruno Mars. Upbeat without demanding attention.
  2. “Can't Stop the Feeling!” — Justin Timberlake. Safe at any age.
  3. “Best Day of My Life” — American Authors. Sunny background energy.
  4. “Walking on Sunshine” — Katrina & The Waves. Cheerful and familiar.
  5. “Good Life” — OneRepublic. Easy filler that nobody objects to.
  6. “Shut Up and Dance” — WALK THE MOON. Bright and clean.
  7. “Feel It Still” — Portugal. The Man. Light, quick, unobtrusive.
  8. “Hey Ya!” — OutKast. Recognizable and harmless.
  9. “Dynamite” — BTS. Bright, clean, current.
  10. “Cake by the Ocean” — DNCE. Upbeat — use the clean edit.

Sound effects: home runs, strikeouts, and the third out

Effects are the cheapest win in amateur ball. A horn on a home run, a swing-and-miss sting on a strikeout, a short cheer on the third out — each is a second long and each one makes the moment land. They also cover the awkward gap while a kid rounds the bases grinning.

Two cautions. Do not play an effect on the opposing team's mistakes; it reads badly and umpires notice. And keep the set small — four or five effects used consistently beat twenty used at random.

What you are actually allowed to play

There is no national governing body that publishes a rule about music between innings. Not for youth baseball, not for youth softball. What governs your field is entirely local: your league board, the park district or school that owns the facility, and any noise ordinance that covers the site.

In practice that means the answer differs from league to league, and some leagues do not allow music during games at all. Ask your board before you invest a season's effort into it. If anyone raises music licensing for public performance, that is a question for the league or the facility that owns the field rather than something a coach or an app settles — venues, not individuals, are normally the ones who hold those licences.

Canadian coaches: the same applies at minor ball and house league diamonds, where the municipality or school that owns the field usually sets the rules on amplified sound. Ask the association before the season rather than the week of a tournament.
